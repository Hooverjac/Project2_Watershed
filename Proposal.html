From Community Participation to Financial Support

Proposal

library(tidyverse)

Goal

The goal of this project is to investigate how effectively non-monetary community events are associated with converting participants into financial supporters of a nonprofit organization, specifically Watershed Management Group (WMG). Nonprofits frequently organize events to increase community engagement, but it is not always clear whether this engagement translates into long-term financial support. These were problems brought to me directly by upper-management at WMG and I thought it would be fun to explore in this project.

Using historical event participation and donation records, I hope to identify patterns in donor conversion and retention. Ultimately, I want to develop visualizations that can help WMG better understand the relationship between community engagement and financial giving, potentially informing future decisions about event planning and donor outreach.

This project will use two datasets exported from Watershed Management Group’s CiviCRM database. WMG uses CiviCRM to track community event participation and financial contributions.

The first dataset contains 60105 event participation records across 9 variables, representing 22631 unique contacts. These records include information such as event titles, event dates, participant statuses, and participant identifiers.

The second dataset contains 38020 financial contribution records across 11 variables, representing 8321 unique contacts. Variables include contribution dates, donation amounts, contribution statuses, and contribution identifiers.

Both datasets contain a shared Contact ID variable, allowing event participation records to be connected with financial contributions. An initial comparison identified 6210 contacts appearing in both datasets, representing approximately 27.44% of all event contacts.

Questions

Question 1: What percentage of individuals who attend free community events become first-time financial donors within 12 months of their initial event attendance?

Question 2: How is community event participation associated with long-term financial support, particularly repeat donations and donor retention?

Analysis plan

Question 1: Event Participation and Donor Conversion

To answer the first question, I will merge the event participation and contribution datasets using their shared Contact ID variable.

I will first clean the datasets by standardizing dates, reviewing missing values, and filtering participation records to include individuals marked as “Attended.” I will also exclude future events and identify which events qualify as non-monetary community events.

Next, I will identify each individual’s first recorded event attendance and determine whether they had made a financial contribution before attending. Individuals who donated previously will be classified as existing donors rather than new donors.

For individuals without prior donations, I will calculate whether they made their first financial contribution within 365 days of their initial attendance. I will restrict this calculation to individuals with a complete 12-month follow-up period.

The main variables will include Contact ID, Event ID, Event Title, Event Start Date, Participant Status, Contribution ID, and Contribution Date.

New variables will include first attendance date, first donation date, prior donor status, days between attendance and donation, and conversion within 12 months (Yes/No).

I plan to create a bar chart showing the percentage of eligible event attendees who became first-time donors compared with those who did not. I may also compare conversion rates across different years or event categories.

Question 2: Event Participation and Long-Term Support

To answer the second question, I will examine whether individuals who participate in more community events demonstrate different donation patterns than those who attend fewer events.

Using the merged datasets, I will calculate the number of events attended by each individual, the number of completed contributions they made, and their total donation amounts.

I will also identify donors who made multiple contributions and examine whether they donated again within 12 months of their first donation. Comparisons will use consistent observation periods where possible.

The main variables will include Contact ID, Event ID, Event Start Date, Contribution ID, Contribution Date, and Total Amount.

New variables will include total events attended, total contributions, total donation amount, repeat donor status (Yes/No), and donation retention within 12 months (Yes/No).

I plan to use bar charts to compare repeat donation rates across event participation groups and line charts to visualize donation activity over time.

Because the data is observational, this analysis will focus on identifying associations rather than establishing that event participation directly causes financial contributions.

Planned visualizations

I plan to create several visualizations to explore the relationship between event participation and financial contributions.

Figure 1: Event Attendance and First-Time Donor Conversion

A bar chart showing the percentage of eligible event attendees who made their first financial contribution within 12 months of attending an event, compared with those who did not. This visualization will help establish the overall conversion rate among individuals who had not previously donated.

Figure 2: Donor Conversion Over Time

A line chart showing the percentage of eligible first-time attendees who became donors within 12 months, grouped by the year of their initial event attendance. This will help identify whether conversion rates have changed over time and whether particular years demonstrate unusually high or low conversion rates.

Figure 3: Event Participation and Repeat Donations

A bar chart comparing the percentage of donors who made repeat contributions across different event attendance groups, such as individuals who attended one event, two events, or three or more events. This visualization will help examine whether more frequent community engagement is associated with continued financial support.

If the available data permits, I may also examine differences across event categories to identify which types of community engagement are associated with higher conversion rates.

Potential challenges

Several challenges may affect the analysis.

Distinguishing attendance from registration: The event participation dataset contains 60,105 records, but only 26,571 are explicitly marked as “Attended.” Including individuals who registered but did not attend could misrepresent the relationship between community engagement and financial giving. I will therefore focus primarily on confirmed attendance records and investigate whether attendance was recorded consistently across the years.

Identifying first-time donors: Some individuals may have made financial contributions before attending their first recorded event. These individuals should not be considered new donor conversions. I will use the historical contribution records to identify prior donors and distinguish them from individuals who donated for the first time after attending an event.

Historical data completeness: Although event records date back to 2011 and contribution records date back to 2006, the completeness of historical records may vary. Additionally, more recent event attendees may not have had sufficient time to make a contribution within the proposed 12-month observation period. I will account for these limitations when selecting the analysis period.

Defining event categories: The dataset contains event titles but does not provide a consistently populated event type variable. This may make it difficult to distinguish between different types of community events. I may need to classify events using their titles and confirm those classifications with the organization.

Interpreting associations: Individuals who attend multiple events may already be more engaged with the organization than those who attend only one. Therefore, any relationship between attendance frequency and donor retention cannot necessarily be interpreted as a causal effect.
